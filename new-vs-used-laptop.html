Buying

New vs refurbished laptops for your office: an honest comparison

RMG Tech SolutionsUpdated 2026

We supply both new and refurbished laptops, so we have no reason to push either. What matters is matching the machine to the desk, the work and the risk.

Sound familiar?

  • You bought the cheapest laptops for the team, and now several are out for service at the same time?
  • You paid for new laptops for interns who only use email and Excel?
  • You received refurbished units with no proper invoice and no record of the warranty?

Choose the right machine for each desk, and you protect both your budget and your team's working time.

The case for refurbished

A refurbished business-class laptop — Lenovo ThinkPad, Dell Latitude, HP EliteBook — was built for corporate use. Companies buy these in thousands, use them for three or four years, and then they enter the refurbished market at a fraction of their original price.

For that money you get build quality no new budget laptop matches: better keyboards, stronger hinges, easy servicing and spare parts that are easy to find. For staff who type all day, a business keyboard alone makes a difference.

The case for new

The refurbished market has no fixed price and no referee. The battery may be at 60% of its original capacity, the SSD may be worn, and the processor is a few generations old. Units older than Intel 8th gen are not supported by Windows 11 at all.

And the biggest difference: warranty. A seller warranty of a few months is not the same as a one- to three-year onsite manufacturer warranty. For staff whose downtime costs the business money, that warranty is worth paying for.

Our rule of thumb

Our recommendationBUY NEW ......... finance, management and client-facing staff, and anyone who needs zero-tension use for 3+ years BUY REFURBISHED .. training rooms, computer labs, interns, spare and backup units, short projects

The checks we run on every refurbished laptop

If you buy refurbished from anyone, insist on these checks before you pay. An honest supplier will let you run them. If a seller refuses, that refusal is your answer.

  1. SSD health. Run CrystalDiskInfo. Health should say "Good", ideally above 85%. See our SSD health guide.
  2. Battery report. Run powercfg /batteryreport and compare Design Capacity with Full Charge Capacity. Below 60% means a replacement is due soon. See our battery guide.
  3. Keyboard. Open Notepad and press every key. Ex-office laptops sometimes have worn or dead keys.
  4. Screen. Open a full-white and a full-black image. Look for dead pixels, lines and pressure marks.
  5. Hinges and body. Open and close the lid slowly. Grinding, cracking or a wobbling screen means damaged hinge mounts.
  6. Ports and charging. Test every USB port with a pen drive, and gently wiggle the charger plug. A loose charging port is a common fault on used laptops.
  7. Processor generation. Intel 8th gen or newer for Windows 11 support.
  8. BIOS and company lock. Confirm the BIOS opens without a password and Windows sets up without asking for another company's login. Our ThinkPad guide explains why.

What about "certified refurbished" online?

Certified refurbished from a proper platform sits between new and shop-refurbished: you get a return window and some warranty. For a one-off purchase it is a reasonable path; read the condition grade carefully. For a team, a local supplier who gives a GST invoice, replaces a faulty unit the next day and can service the machines on site is usually less trouble.

Whichever way you go, a laptop is a work tool. The best one is the one your staff never have to think about.

Common questions

Are refurbished laptops reliable enough for office use?

Business-class models such as ThinkPad, Latitude and EliteBook are, when they are 8th gen Intel or newer and each unit is checked: SSD health, battery, keyboard, screen, hinges, ports and BIOS lock.
